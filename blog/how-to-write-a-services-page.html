How to Write a Services Page That Gets Enquiries (UK Guide)

Someone's sat on the sofa with a cup of tea, trying to work out if a loft conversion is a fantasy or a plan. They find your website and tap Services.

The page says "Extensions. Lofts. Renovations. Quality workmanship guaranteed." That's it. No idea of cost, no idea what's included, no idea what happens next.

They close the tab and go back to Rightmove. Here's how to write a services page that does the opposite, using two of our concept sites as worked examples.

Write for the question in their head, not the list in yours

Most services pages are written from the owner's side of the counter. A list of things you do, in the order you thought of them.

The customer arrives with a different list. Can you do my job? Roughly how much? What do I actually get? How long will it take? What do I do now?

A good services page answers those, in that order, for every service. That's the whole trick. Everything below is just how to do it without writing an essay.

Give every service its own block

Lumping everything into one paragraph forces people to dig. Give each service a block with the same five parts:

  1. A plain name. "End of tenancy clean", not "Property transition solutions".
  2. One or two sentences on who it's for. "A full top-to-bottom reset for your whole home."
  3. A starting price. Even a rough "from" figure.
  4. What's included, as three or four short bullet points.
  5. A button that's specific to that service. "Quote for extensions" beats a generic "Contact us".

Same layout every time means people can skim. They spot the service they want and every answer is in the same place.

Put a price on it, even a rough one

This is where most small business pages go quiet. We get it: every job is different, and you don't want to be held to a number before you've seen the job.

A "from" price solves that. It tells a customer whether they're in the right ballpark, and it puts off the people who were never going to pay what the job costs. That saves you a site visit you'd never have won.

If the work genuinely can't be priced in advance, say so and explain how you price it: "Priced per project. We visit, measure and send an itemised quote." That's still an answer. We wrote more about why hidden prices hurt in Why "DM for Price" Is Costing You Customers.

A walkthrough: our Corbel Build concept site

Corbel Build Co. is a concept site we built to show what a builder's website could look like. It's not a real business, and the prices on it are illustrations, not quotes. The services page is a decent model for any trade, though.

Look at what that one block does:

  • The price comes first. "From £45,000" sits above the heading, so nobody has to hunt for it.
  • The description is one sentence of benefit. Space, light and value, then done.
  • The bullets deal with worries. "Planning and building regs handled for you" answers the thing that keeps people up at night before they've even asked.
  • The button names the service. "Quote for extensions" tells the builder what the enquiry is about before he's opened it.

The page repeats that pattern for lofts, renovations, new builds and commercial fit-outs. New builds have no "from" figure, so the label says "Priced per project" instead of leaving a gap.

There's also a row of buttons at the top for each service. On a long page, that saves someone scrolling past four things they don't want to reach the one they do.

Answer the awkward questions before they ask

The bottom of the Corbel page has four FAQs, and they're not filler. They're the questions a builder hears on every first visit.

Notice "Is your quote really fixed?" in there. That's the fear behind most building enquiries, and answering it plainly does more than any amount of "trusted local builder" copy.

The panel underneath tells people exactly what happens next: a reply within one working day and a visit within a week. Setting that expectation stops people ringing three other firms "just in case".

Pull your own FAQs from real life. Scroll back through your texts and DMs and write down the questions you've answered more than twice.

Make extras easy to add

Our Brightside Cleaning concept site shows another trick worth pinching: add-ons with their own prices.

The three main cleans each list what's included, and the "Most popular" label nudges the undecided towards the middle option. Underneath, the add-ons turn "can you also do the oven?" from a text conversation into something a customer picks themselves.

If your trade has natural extras (a dog hair surcharge for a valeter, a wash and blow-dry on top of a cut), price them on the page. You'll stop doing them for free because it felt awkward to mention.

Real businesses, same rules

Case study

OTR Detailing

Mobile car valeting and detailing, Coventry

The business: Gideon runs OTR Detailing on his own and comes to customers' houses around Coventry.

What we built: his services are on the site as three packages with prices (£20 exterior, £30 interior, £40 full), and each one spells out exactly what's included. No "prices on request".

Why it's relevant here: it's the same block structure as the Corbel page, scaled down for a one-person business. The site is new, so we've got no enquiry figures to share, and we won't make any up. You can read the full story in our case study on OTR Detailing and Styled By KY.

Styled By KY, a hair stylist in Derby, uses the same idea at a bigger scale: a full price list for ladies, gents and locs, broken down by length, so clients can work out the price before they message.

Words that cost you enquiries

A few phrases turn up on services pages all the time, and none of them tell a customer anything:

  • "Quality workmanship." Everyone says it. Show a photo of the work instead.
  • "No job too small." Fine, but tell people what the smallest job you'll actually do costs.
  • "Competitive prices." Competitive with what? Give a number.
  • "Contact us for more information." About what? Name the next step.

Write like you'd explain it to a customer at the door. Short sentences, real numbers, no brochure voice.

Do this today

  1. List your services on paper and cross out anything you don't actually want more of. Your services page should sell the work you like.
  2. Write a "from" price next to each one, or one line on how you price it.
  3. Add three or four bullets per service saying what's included.
  4. Collect your five most-asked questions from your texts and DMs, and answer each in two sentences.
  5. Change every "Contact us" button to something specific, like "Quote for a deep clean".
  6. Add one line on what happens next, such as when you reply and whether you visit first.

FAQ

What should be on a services page?

Each service should have a clear name, a short description, a starting price or how you price it, what's included, and a button to enquire about that service. Add an FAQ and a line about what happens after someone gets in touch.

Should I put prices on my services page?

Usually, yes. A "from" price helps customers decide if you're right for them and filters out enquiries you'd never win. If a job can't be priced in advance, explain how you quote instead.

Should each service have its own page?

If you offer a few services, one well-organised page is fine. If a service is a big earner or something people search for on its own, such as "loft conversions Leicester", a separate page gives it more room to rank on Google.

How long should a services page be?

Long enough to answer the questions people ask before they get in touch. For most small businesses that's a short block per service plus a handful of FAQs, not paragraphs of sales copy.
